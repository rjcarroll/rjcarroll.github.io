Grad Notes

A growing collection of self-contained handouts on the mathematics needed to read formal political-economy theory and to write it. In the spirit of Kim Border’s mathematical economics notes, but oriented to political scientists, with examples drawn from across political science and political economy. Each handout is independent and reference-able on its own; the numbering below is a suggested reading order rather than a strict prerequisite chain.

Foundations

Language and logic

The first formal vocabulary the project assumes — propositions, connectives, valuations, and what follows from what.

  • Preface

    Project framing, conventions, scope, and what is assumed. Background: algebra and a year of calculus.

  • Propositional logic

    The simplest formal system in which the notion of “follows from” can be made precise. Syntax (atomic propositions, connectives, well-formed formulas), semantics (valuations, truth-in-a-valuation), tautology / contradiction / satisfiability, logical equivalence, and logical consequence. PE examples: bicameralism with veto, contraposition in the democratic peace, denying the antecedent as a fallacy.

Sets and structures

Sets and the basic structural vocabulary built on them — set operations, relations, orders, and cardinality.

  • Naive set theory

    Sets, set operations and the Venn-diagram picture, indexed unions and intersections, Cartesian products and tuples, disjoint unions, relations and equivalence classes, functions, and a closing footnote on Russell’s paradox and how the axiom of separation rules it out.

  • Order theory

    Preorders, partial orders, and total orders, with the strict-and-symmetric decomposition; Hasse diagrams; bounds, maxima vs. suprema, and the maximum-versus-maximal distinction; lattices; order-preserving functions, with the headline application being the utility representation of preferences (and the canonical lex-preferences-have-no-utility-representation counterexample).

  • Cardinality and infinity

    Comparing the sizes of infinite sets via bijection; Schröder–Bernstein; the countability of \(\mathbb{Z}\), \(\mathbb{Q}\), and \(\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}\); Cantor’s diagonal argument for the uncountability of \(\mathbb{R}\); Cantor’s theorem and the unbounded hierarchy of cardinals; the discrete-versus-continuous translation that organizes a great deal of applied modeling.

Analysis

Real analysis on \(\mathbb{R}^n\) — the structural backbone of every existence-and-comparative-statics argument in formal political-economy theory.

  • Sequences and limits

    \(\epsilon\)-\(N\) convergence; the least upper bound property as the structural fact distinguishing \(\mathbb{R}\) from \(\mathbb{Q}\); the construction of \(\mathbb{R}\) from \(\mathbb{Q}\) as an extended footnote; the completeness-equivalence triangle (LUB / monotone convergence / Cauchy / Bolzano–Weierstrass); and \(\limsup\) / \(\liminf\) for bounded sequences that don’t converge.

  • Open and closed sets

    \(\mathbb{R}^n\) with the Euclidean metric; open and closed sets and their algebra; closure, interior, boundary, accumulation points, with the sequential characterization of closedness; compactness via open covers, Heine–Borel, and the sequential equivalent, with a footnote on the failure of “closed and bounded = compact” in infinite dimensions; connectedness in \(\mathbb{R}\) (= intervals) and convex-implies-connected in \(\mathbb{R}^n\).

  • Continuity

    \(\epsilon\)-\(\delta\) continuity at a point with the sequential and topological characterizations; the algebra of continuous functions; the intermediate value theorem (the topological reason market-clearing arguments work); the extreme value theorem (the topological reason optimization problems have solutions); uniform continuity and the Heine–Cantor theorem.

Probability and measure

Measure-theoretic probability, from \(\sigma\)-algebras through the central limit theorem — the foundations underlying polling, learning, and any stochastic model.

  • Probability spaces and measures

    \(\sigma\)-algebras (the right collection of subsets to assign probabilities to, given that the full power set \(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})\) is too rich to admit a translation-invariant countably additive measure); measures and the Kolmogorov axioms; basic measure properties (monotonicity, subadditivity, continuity from above and below); Lebesgue measure on \(\mathbb{R}\); independence, conditional probability, and Bayes’ rule.

  • Integration

    Riemann integration with rigor (the partition definition, the FTC, the Dirichlet-function failure of Riemann integrability); Lebesgue integration built in three stages on a measure space; monotone convergence, Fatou’s lemma, dominated convergence; the Riemann–Lebesgue agreement theorem and Lebesgue’s characterization of Riemann-integrability (“bounded and continuous almost everywhere”).

  • Random variables and expectations

    Random variables as measurable functions; distributions and CDFs (discrete and absolutely continuous); expectation as the Lebesgue integral against the probability measure; variance and covariance; the standard inequalities (Markov, Chebyshev, Jensen, Cauchy–Schwarz); conditional expectation via Radon–Nikodym, with the tower property as the workhorse identity.

  • Convergence and limit theorems

    The four modes of convergence (almost sure, in probability, in \(L^p\), in distribution) and their logical relations, with extended footnote on the converse counterexamples; the Borel–Cantelli lemmas; the weak and strong laws of large numbers; the classical i.i.d. central limit theorem, with the survey margin-of-error worked example explaining why a sample of \(n = 1000\) produces a \(\pm 3\)-percentage-point confidence band.

Formal logic, deeper

Proof systems, first-order logic, and the model-theoretic vocabulary in which formal political-economy modeling already implicitly operates.

  • Proof systems

    Natural deduction with introduction and elimination rules and assumption discharge, in Gentzen-style horizontal-line presentation; sequent calculus and Hilbert systems noted in passing; soundness and completeness for propositional logic via Lindenbaum’s lemma and the canonical-valuation construction; compactness as a corollary of completeness.

  • First-order logic

    Signatures, terms, formulas (free vs. bound, sentences); structures and Tarski’s recursive definition of satisfaction; many-sorted FOL with sorted variables and structures-with-multiple-carriers — the natural setting for political-economy modeling, where the universe of discourse is typed (voters, candidates, strategies, alternatives) rather than a single undifferentiated set; Gödel’s completeness theorem stated and contrasted with the incompleteness theorems.

  • Model theory and modeling

    Theories as sets of sentences, the \(\mathrm{Mod}\) / \(\mathrm{Th}\) Galois correspondence, elementary equivalence and isomorphism, complete theories, and the semantic view of theories (Suppes, van Fraassen) — and how it lines up with the way political scientists already use the word “model”: every Cournot duopoly, agenda-setter model, signaling game is a structure or class of structures in this sense.

Linear algebra

Linear algebra over \(\mathbb{R}\) — vector spaces, linear maps, eigenvalues, quadratic forms — the foundation under spatial-voting models, network analysis, Markov chains, and the second-order conditions of optimization.

  • Vector spaces and linear maps

    Vector spaces with \(\mathbb{R}^n\), function spaces \(\mathbb{R}^X\), and polynomials as canonical examples (and indicator vectors as a deliberately-not-subspace case); linear independence, span, basis, dimension (with the well-definedness theorem); linear maps with kernel, image, and the rank-nullity theorem; matrices as encodings of linear maps under chosen bases (with matrix multiplication as composition, change of basis as conjugation); inner products, Cauchy–Schwarz, Gram–Schmidt, orthogonal projection. Two extended footnotes: dimension and degrees-of-freedom in PE models (the empirical “how many dimensions of conflict” question — NOMINATE finding two, Plott chaos in higher dimensions); and inner products as similarity (quadratic-loss spatial-voting utility unpacked as an inner-product calculation, “centrist” and “ideologically close” as inner-product statements).

  • Eigenvalue theory and quadratic forms

    Eigenvalues and eigenvectors with the characteristic-polynomial criterion; diagonalization (with the defective-matrix and Jordan caveat); symmetric matrices and the spectral theorem (real eigenvalues, orthonormal eigenbasis); quadratic forms and definiteness, with eigenvalue-based characterization and Sylvester’s criterion via leading principal minors; stochastic matrices and the Perron–Frobenius theorem with the irreducibility and aperiodicity refinement that delivers Markov-chain convergence to a unique stationary distribution. Two extended footnotes: the spectral theorem and self-adjointness (real symmetric vs. Hermitian, normal vs. defective, with PCA and SVD as the structural payoff); and definiteness and concavity (the bordered-Hessian preview for the optimization handouts to come). PE: voter learning as a linear iteration, eigenvector centrality on political networks, a worked two-party voter-migration Markov chain.

Optimization

Optimization on \(\mathbb{R}^n\) — the differentiation, convexity, static, and dynamic apparatus that runs under every comparative-statics, equilibrium-existence, and dynamic-policy argument in formal political-economy theory.

  • Differentiation in \(\mathbb{R}^n\)

    Partial derivatives and the gradient with the directional-derivative formula and the gradient-as-direction-of-steepest-ascent reading; total derivatives, Jacobians, the chain rule; higher-order derivatives and Hessians with Clairaut’s symmetry-of-mixed-partials theorem; Taylor’s theorem to second order as the local-quadratic-approximation underlying SOCs; the implicit function theorem with the explicit comparative-statics formula \(D \mathbf{g} = -[D_{\mathbf{y}} \mathbf{F}]^{-1} D_{\mathbf{x}} \mathbf{F}\), set up as the formal engine of comparative statics in equilibrium analysis. Two extended footnotes: gradients perpendicular to level sets and the spatial-voting indifference-curve reading (with Plott-style chaos as a gradient-geometry argument); and the IFT as the structural engine of comparative statics (with Milgrom–Shannon and Topkis monotone-comparative-statics theory as the differentiability-free alternative).

  • Convex sets and concave functions

    Convex sets and the canonical PE convex sets (the probability simplex, mixed-strategy spaces, budget sets, intersections); convex hull; the separating hyperplane theorem with the supporting-hyperplane corollary; concave and convex functions, with the local-implies-global maximum theorem proved directly; the first-order (gradient-inequality) and second-order (Hessian-negative-semidefinite) characterizations of concavity, leaning on the eigenvalue-and-Sylvester machinery of the previous handout; quasi-concavity with the upper-contour-set definition, concave-implies-quasi-concave, and the bordered-Hessian sufficient condition. Two extended footnotes: the gradient-inequality / supporting-hyperplane reading of concavity (with the FOC-sufficiency corollary); and the bordered-Hessian condition’s awkward-but-standard role in the constrained SOC.

  • Static optimization

    Unconstrained optimization with FOC and SOC, leveraging the previous handouts’ Hessian and local-implies-global theorems; equality-constrained problems via Lagrange multipliers, with the shadow-price interpretation worked out; inequality-constrained problems via the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions and complementary slackness; existence theorems (Weierstrass via compactness from the analysis cluster) and Berge’s theorem of the maximum (correspondences, upper-/lower-hemicontinuity, the value function’s continuity in parameters), framed as the structural backbone of equilibrium-existence arguments in game theory; the envelope theorem (unconstrained and constrained) as the engine of comparative statics. Two extended footnotes: Lagrange multipliers as shadow prices (with the constraint-qualification cautionary note); and the envelope theorem and monotone comparative statics (Milgrom–Shannon, Topkis-style supermodularity arguments). PE: spatial-voter unconstrained maximization, coalition allocating a fixed budget across districts, politician with a budget cap and a poll-position constraint, best-response correspondence via Berge for game-theoretic equilibrium existence, comparative statics under campaign-effectiveness shifts.

  • Dynamic optimization

    Finite-horizon dynamic programming and the Bellman recursion (with backward induction); infinite-horizon discounted problems with stationary value function and stationary optimal policy; the Bellman equation recast as the fixed-point equation \(V^* = T V^*\) for the Bellman operator \(T\) on bounded functions under the sup norm; the contraction mapping theorem of Banach with proof sketch, and the proposition that the Bellman operator is a contraction with modulus \(\beta\) — the structural reason value-function iteration converges from any starting guess; the envelope condition (Benveniste–Scheinkman) as the dynamic analogue of the static envelope theorem. Two extended footnotes: Banach’s broader role across mathematical economics (the IFT proof, Picard–Lindelöf for ODEs, equilibrium-existence under strategic complementarities); and time consistency under non-geometric discounting (Strotz 1955, Kydland–Prescott 1977 on optimal monetary and fiscal policy as a commitment problem). PE: legislative bargaining over a four-period session, voter learning over infinitely many elections under Bayesian updating, comparative statics under campaign-effectiveness shifts via the dynamic envelope, authoritarian-regime survival as a stochastic Bellman problem.

Markov chains

Markov chains as the natural framework for stochastic political-economy dynamics whose future depends on the past only through the present, plus Markov decision processes as the natural extension to stochastic dynamic-decision settings.

  • Markov chains and Markov decision processes

    Markov chains and the Markov property on a finite or countable state space, transition matrices, \(n\)-step probabilities, Chapman–Kolmogorov; reachability, communicating classes, irreducibility, periodicity, recurrence, with the finite-chain theorem that closed communicating classes are recurrent; stationary distributions and the existence/uniqueness/convergence theorem (proved via Perron–Frobenius from the eigenvalue handout), with the spectral gap and the geometric-rate convergence proposition; reversibility, detailed balance, and mixing time, with the spectral-gap-based mixing-time bound for reversible chains; Markov decision processes as Markov chains with decisions, the stochastic Bellman equation, and the proposition that the stochastic Bellman operator is a contraction with modulus \(\beta\) — the same Banach argument as the dynamic-optimization handout, with the deterministic continuation \(V^*(f(s, a))\) replaced by the conditional-expected continuation \(\sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a) V^*(s')\). Two extended footnotes: general-state-space convergence and MCMC (Doeblin / Harris recurrence, with MCMC as the structural backbone of modern Bayesian political methodology — ideal-point estimation, DW-NOMINATE, IRT, structural topic models all leaning on it); and Markov-perfect equilibrium as the strategic generalization of MDPs (Maskin–Tirole, with appearances throughout dynamic political economy: dynamic legislative bargaining, dynamic political agency, dynamic models of authoritarian survival, electoral competition with state variables). PE: two-party generational party-affiliation chain, opinion dynamics with absorbing extremes, polling-and-mixing applications, authoritarian-regime survival as a stochastic Bellman problem.

Categories

Category theory as a structural language. Two handouts oriented toward the parts a working political economist will encounter: the universal-property style of definition, and the Yoneda lemma’s “an object is determined by its maps in” slogan.

  • Categories and functors

    Categories with the basic data (objects, morphisms, composition, identity) and the canonical examples (\(\mathbf{Set}\), \(\mathbf{Pos}\), \(\mathbf{Top}\), \(\mathbf{Meas}\), preorders as thin categories, monoids as one-object categories); iso / mono / epi, with the structuralist moral that “one-to-one” presupposes elements while mono asks only for cancellation under composition — making mono the right concept whenever the units are bracketed (states-as-units in IR theory, aggregate actors in organizational economics); universal properties (initial / terminal / products / coproducts), with the meet-and-join translation in thin categories; functors with examples (forgetful, power-set, hom-functors, voting-rule-as-functor); equivalence of categories. Two extended footnotes: set-of-all-sets vs. category-of-all-categories (size hygiene as the categorical analogue of Russell’s paradox); and the iso/mono/epi vs. injective/surjective/bijective mismatches in non-Set categories.

  • Natural transformations and the Yoneda lemma

    Natural transformations and naturality squares; the pointwise-inequality reading in thin categories; functor categories — which finally make precise the “isomorphism of functors” that the previous handout’s equivalence-of-categories definition was leaning on; hom-functors and representable functors; the Yoneda lemma with full proof; the Yoneda embedding \(\mathcal{C} \hookrightarrow [\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}, \mathbf{Set}]\) and the slogan “an object is determined by its maps in” made precise. Closes with a suggestive (and honestly hedged) revealed-preference echo: an alternative is determined by the menus on which it is chosen.

Decision Theory

Individual choice over deterministic alternatives, over lotteries with given probabilities, and over acts when the probabilities themselves are not pinned down — the three escalating positions on the epistemic state of the chooser.

  • Choice under certainty

    Choice functions and choice correspondences on finite menus, with budget families as the cross-menu observation structure; rationalizability; revealed weak and strict preference; WARP and the Arrow–Sen rationalization theorem; SARP and Houthakker’s theorem; the preference–utility–choice triangle as the synthesis section, with each arrow’s failure mode named (lex preferences for \(\succsim \to u\), empty argmax for \(u \to C\), Condorcet cycle for \(C \to \succsim\)). PE: primary voter, agenda setter, Sen’s \(\alpha/\beta\) committee failure, Condorcet cycles as population-level WARP failure (the operational version of Arrow’s impossibility caught by the diagnostic axioms).

  • Choice under risk

    Lotteries on a finite outcome space (\(\Delta(X)\) as a simplex); the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms with independence as the load-bearing axiom; the expected-utility theorem with full proof in three steps (best/worst extraction, indifference probability via continuity, linear extension via independence); the Allais paradox as a structural failure of independence; prospect theory (S-shaped value function, probability weighting, loss aversion); rank-dependent expected utility as the EU-respecting weakening that preserves stochastic dominance. Two extended footnotes: what independence is geometrically doing (parallel hyperplanes on the Marschak–Machina triangle, Machina 1982 as the principled smooth-utility weakening); and the prospect-theory reference point (status-quo bias, Quattrone–Tversky 1988 on voters framing relative to status quo).

  • Choice under ambiguity

    Savage’s subjective expected utility framework with the P1–P7 axioms and the representation theorem stated; Anscombe–Aumann acts (functions from states to lotteries) as the lighter axiomatic path that builds on the previous handout’s vNM machinery; the Ellsberg paradox in the three-color version, with the proof that no single prior rationalizes the modal pattern; Knightian uncertainty as the distinction the Bayesian programme had foreclosed and Ellsberg reopened; Gilboa–Schmeidler maxmin EU as the centerpiece, with the Ellsberg-under-maxmin example worked out and a worked climate-policy regulator example; Schmeidler’s Choquet expected utility, with the Choquet–maxmin equivalence theorem on convex capacities tying the two multi-prior frameworks together. Two extended footnotes: Knight, Keynes, and the Bayesian sweep (with the Allais-vs-Ellsberg conceptual depth comparison); uncertainty aversion as the key axiom (with the Hansen–Sargent robust-control connection).

Welfare and Bargaining

Two handouts on the welfare-and-bargaining branch of applied political-economy modeling: efficiency and welfare-function characterizations of Pareto optima, and axiomatic bargaining solutions defined on the Pareto set with disagreement points (Nash, Kalai–Smorodinsky).

  • Pareto optimality and welfare functions

    Pareto dominance as a strict partial order on the alternative space (ordinal, not requiring interpersonal utility comparisons); the two-voter spatial-loss example with the contract curve as the canonical political-economy Pareto-set picture, and the coalition-budget-allocation example showing the Pareto set can be the entire alternative space; existence of Pareto optima under compactness and continuity, via the slick Weierstrass-on-the-unweighted-utilitarian-sum proof; sufficient conditions for Pareto optimality via positive-weight welfare-function maximization, with the strengthening to non-negative weights under strict quasi-concavity; the welfare-function characterization theorem — every Pareto optimum is a non-negative-weighted-sum welfare-function maximizer under convexity and concavity, proved via separating hyperplane on the utility-imputation set \(\mathcal{U}\) and the strictly-dominating cone \(\mathcal{V}\); utilitarianism as the equal-weights special case, Rawlsian maximin, and Bergson–Samuelson welfare functions as the substantive landscape of welfare aggregation; brief discussion of what fails without concavity. Two extended footnotes: the welfare-economics framing of the aggregation question (with Arrow on impossibility, Sen on the Paretian-liberal paradox, and forward-pointers to the social-choice cluster); the more general welfare-function characterization theory beyond linear aggregation (Negishi’s planner’s-problem approach, Mas-Colell on infinite-dimensional generalization, matching theory and mechanism design as the non-convex-case applications). PE: two-voter spatial-loss contract curve, coalition budget allocation with utilitarian FOC, three-voter coalition vs. unanimity, reform vs. status quo as Pareto-improvement.

  • Axiomatic bargaining

    Bargaining problems formalized as a pair \((S, d)\) with \(S\) a non-empty compact convex set of feasible utility profiles and \(d\) a disagreement point strictly Pareto-dominated by some feasible alternative; the bargaining range as the intersection of the Pareto frontier with the individually-rational set; the Fearon (1995) war-bargaining setup running as the canonical example throughout — \(X = [0, 1]\), \(u_A(x) = x\), \(u_B(x) = 1 - x\), \(d = (p - c_A, 1 - p - c_B)\), with bargaining range \([p - c_A, p + c_B]\) of width exactly equal to the sum of war costs; the Nash bargaining solution as the maximizer of \((u_A - d_A)(u_B - d_B)\), with the explicit Fearon-case formula \(x_{\mathfrak{N}} = p + (c_B - c_A)/2\) (the bargained share is the win probability plus half the cost asymmetry); the asymmetric / generalized Nash solution with bargaining weights as the workhorse for asymmetric institutional power; Nash’s axiomatic characterization theorem with the four axioms (Pareto efficiency, symmetry, scale invariance, IIA) and proof sketch; the Kalai–Smorodinsky alternative weakening of IIA in favor of monotonicity, with the geometric reading as the unique Pareto-optimal point on the segment from \(d\) to the ideal point; the welfare-function reading of bargaining solutions as a logarithmic Bergson–Samuelson welfare function anchored at the disagreement point — the structural bridge back to the welfare-function apparatus of the previous handout. Two extended footnotes: the Nash program (axiomatic and strategic foundations of the Nash solution converging via Rubinstein’s alternating-offers limit; the strategic side deferred to the future game-theory cluster); and the Fearon war-bargaining puzzle and its political-economy literature (Powell on commitment problems and shifting power, Fearon on private information, Wagner on bargaining-and-war, Leventoğlu–Tarar on private-information delay). PE: Fearon war-bargaining as the canonical example, asymmetric Nash with institutional power, comparative statics on cost and power (Powell shifting-power reading), legislator–executive budget bargain, union–firm wage bargain with strike costs, KS-vs-Nash divergence on a curved Pareto frontier, and a closing bargaining-failure exercise (Fearon’s three structural sources).

Social Choice

Two handouts on social-choice theory: the impossibility constellation (Arrow, Sen, Wilson, Gibbard–Satterthwaite) under universal domain, and positive results under domain restrictions (single-peakedness, the median voter theorem, Plott / McKelvey chaos in multiple dimensions, single-crossing).

  • Preference aggregation and impossibility

    Social welfare functions \(F : \mathcal{R}^n \to \mathcal{R}\) versus social choice functions \(f : \mathcal{R}^n \to X\), with the housekeeping note on the \(|X| \geq 3\) condition (May 1952’s binary case is structurally special); plurality, Borda, and Condorcet rules as canonical SCFs, with the Condorcet-cycle worked example as the simplest concrete diagnostic of the aggregation problem; Arrow’s four axioms (universal domain, weak Pareto, IIA, non-dictatorship) and the Arrow impossibility theorem with the standard decisive-coalition proof sketch (field-expansion + group-contraction); the substantive interpretation that any aggregation rule with universal domain must give up at least one of (P), (IIA), or (D) — plurality and Borda give up IIA, supermajority gives up Pareto at the margins, dictatorship is the only rule that violates only non-dictatorship; Sen’s Paretian-liberal impossibility (Pareto + minimal liberalism is incompatible under universal domain), with the Lady Chatterley example demonstrating the cycle; Wilson’s theorem (without Pareto, every U+IIA SWF is dictatorial, inverse-dictatorial, or imposed) showing Pareto is not the source of Arrow’s impossibility; Gibbard–Satterthwaite on the SCF side (every strategy-proof full-range SCF on universal domain is dictatorial) with the plurality-manipulation example; the Muller–Satterthwaite link establishing GS as structurally the same impossibility as Arrow’s. Two extended footnotes: housekeeping on May 1952 and the \(|X| \geq 3\) condition; the substantive interpretation of Arrow with Riker’s populist-vs-liberal reading, Sen’s structural-shape constructive reading, and Coleman’s institutions-navigate-impossibility reading. PE: three-reform Condorcet cycle, plurality and Borda IIA-violation, agenda-setter institutions as IIA-violation at the institutional level (with McKelvey 1976 / Riker 1980 forward-references), Lady Chatterley as worked example for Sen’s paradox, plurality strategic-manipulation example for GS, constitutional-convention exercise on which axiom to give up.

  • Domain restrictions and the median voter

    May (1952)’s axiomatic characterization of majority rule on binary alternatives (decisiveness, anonymity, neutrality, positive responsiveness uniquely characterize majority rule, with the substantive observation that supermajority requirements give up neutrality / introduce status-quo bias); single-peaked preferences and Black (1948)’s median voter theorem on one-dimensional ordered alternative spaces (the median voter’s ideal point is the Condorcet winner under single-peakedness, with the no-Condorcet-cycles corollary and the Arrow-relaxes-on-the-single-peaked-domain corollary); the Downsian (1957) two-candidate convergence-to-the-median corollary as the workhorse PE application; single-crossing preferences (Roberts 1977, Gans–Smart 1996) as an alternative domain restriction with the Meltzer–Richard (1981) redistribution model as the canonical PE application; Plott (1967)’s symmetry condition for a multi-dimensional median voter equilibrium and the structural observation that the symmetry is generically fragile; McKelvey (1976, 1979)’s chaos theorem (without Plott’s symmetry, agenda-controlled pairwise voting can move the outcome anywhere in the multi-dimensional alternative space); the political-economy responses to chaos: Shepsle (1979)’s structure-induced equilibrium (institutional decomposition of multi-dimensional voting into one-dimensional sub-decisions) and Riker (1986)’s heresthetic (the political art of manipulating dimensional structure). Two extended footnotes: the broader value-restriction literature (Sen 1966, Inada 1969 characterize the general class of profiles licensing transitive majority rule, with single-peakedness as one specific case); the political-economy implications of McKelvey chaos with SIE and heresthetic as institutional and strategic responses. PE: referendum on a constitutional amendment, five-voter Black’s theorem worked example, Downsian convergence, three-voter single-crossing, Meltzer–Richard redistribution with the median-income voter, Plott-symmetry verification on three-voter \(\mathbb{R}^2\) ideal points, McKelvey chaos in action, agenda-setter exploitation.
